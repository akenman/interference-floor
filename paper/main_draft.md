# Integrator Accuracy, Not Structure: What Determines Closed-Loop Feasibility in Dynamics-Level World Models

*Draft v1 (M2 milestone). Provenance annotations 〔brackets〕 map every number to its JSON source; they become an appendix table at M6. Figure placeholders [Fig n] refer to the plan in TMLR_WRITING_FRAMEWORK.md §3.*

---

## Abstract

Learned world models are trained on one-step prediction and then judged by closed-loop rollout, yet the factor that decides whether they survive that transition is rarely measured. We isolate three candidates — the fidelity of a physics prior's integrator, the training recipe, and symbolic structure — in two simulated worlds, under a preregistered protocol with paired seeds, guard tests that bind every published number to a script, and a measured run-to-run noise floor. Three results stand. First, the prior's computational fidelity sets the floor. Swapping the prior's integrator for the one that generated the data, inside a fixed residual architecture, lowers closed-loop error from 14.26 px to 0.49 px in a pendulum world, and the exact prior reproduces the ball engine to 3×10⁻⁵ px per step and 1.4×10⁻⁴ px over five closed-loop steps; a 401k-parameter learned model matches this floor but does not beat it. The result replicates across engines and data seeds. Second, the recipe multiplies within the floor. A rollout loss repairs an unstable arm by 41× but leaves the exact-prior arm unchanged, and over-weighting the single-step loss eventually reverses closed-loop performance. Symbolic structure subtracts: gated and label-supervised variants lose accuracy across four experiment generations, and the harm vanishes once the floor is exact. Third, the trained arm sits on an interference floor. Its residual head emits noise where the prior is already exact — the supervision there is zero, the output is not — and this noise erases the analytic ladder and compounds in closed loop. Five interventions trace a single false-silence-versus-damage trade-off, and a decode-time hard gate restores parity with the analytic prior at a measured two-percent false-silence rate. We release preregistrations, guard tests, and per-number provenance.

## 1. Introduction

A world model that is meant to support control has to survive closed-loop rollout: the model consumes its own predictions, step after step, and errors compound. The standard workflow trains on one-step prediction, reports a one-step metric, and hopes. The question this paper asks is narrower than "are world models useful". It is: given a fixed architecture family and a fixed data budget, what decides whether a learned model survives the step from one-step fitting to closed-loop rollout?

Three answers circulate in practice. The first is architectural: give the model structure — gating, phase variables, symbolic priors — and it will roll out better. The second is the training recipe: add a rollout loss, tune the schedule. The third is rarely treated as a measured variable at all: the fidelity of an analytic prior's *computation class*, by which we mean the pair of equations and the numerical scheme (integrator, step size, sampling) used to evaluate them. A prior can be exact as mathematics and still be wrong as code, if its discrete map differs from the map that produced the data. A reader may object that, in simulation, matching the generating integrator is trivially the right thing to do. It is the right thing, and it is not trivial to know what to match: our ball engine's documentation specifies a different integrator from the one its code implements (§3.1), and the discrepancy is larger than every ladder gap we resolve except the coarsest.

Measuring this properly takes an instrument, so we built one. Two simulated worlds — an underactuated double pendulum and a bouncing-ball arena — with a shared architecture family in which the residual head, the trunk, and the data budget are held fixed while the prior is swapped through a registry of four integrator tiers. Eleven preregistrations, written before the runs, fix the criteria. Every number in this paper is reproduced by a script from a stored JSON, checked by guard tests, and accompanied by a measured run-to-run noise floor (≤5% one-step, ≤9% closed-loop) below which we do not claim differences.

The findings are ordered by how much they surprised us. The prior ladder is monotonic and steep: in the pendulum world, exchanging the coarse integrator of the prior for the one that generated the data moves the trained arm's one-step error from 0.969 px to 0.037 px and closed-loop error from 14.26 px to 0.49 px — factors of 26 and 29 with the architecture held fixed. The same experiment in the ball world reproduces analytic predictions to four decimal places, and the exact prior there is not merely close to the engine: over five closed-loop steps it *is* the engine, to 3×10⁻⁵ px per step. A learned model with 401k parameters matches this floor and cannot improve on it. The recipe, meanwhile, multiplies within the floor: a rollout loss repairs a collapsing arm by 41× but changes the exact-prior arm by nothing, and there is a dose above which more single-step loss reverses closed-loop performance outright. Symbolic structure subtracts in every form we tried — gated experts across four experiment generations, and, in a two-by-two that separates supervision from capacity, the human-labelled supervision alone costs 0.269 px (6/6 seeds) — yet when the floor is exact, that harm is absorbed almost perfectly (ratio 0.0005).

The trained arm then adds a complication that the analytic ladder cannot show. On the windows where the exact prior is already perfect, the residual head is supposed to output zero; the supervision there is zero to four decimals, and the head's output is not. It emits roughly 0.85 px of noise, the same amount for every prior tier, and this noise erases the analytic ladder in the trained arms and compounds under feedback. We tried five interventions across four preregistrations — loss reweighting, an end-to-end gate, a supervised gate, a loss-mass dose curve, and decode-time hard gating. Each moved the error somewhere else. All of them, together with the decode rule, trace one curve: a trade between silencing the head on free-flight windows and damaging contact windows. The decode rule is the useful end of that curve — it restores closed-loop parity with the analytic prior, at a quoted rate of two-percent false silence.

This paper is a study of two simulated worlds, not a benchmark. The scale is deliberately small and the claims are scoped accordingly. What the small scale buys is control: every factor that could move the numbers is either fixed, preregistered, or guarded by a test. Our contributions:

- **C1 — The floor.** Prior computational fidelity, not architecture, sets closed-loop feasibility. A zero-parameter prior with the engine's integrator is bit-exact in closed loop and ties a 401k-parameter learned model (§4).
- **C2 — The multiplier.** The recipe's gain is proportional to the distance to the floor and saturates there; the dose has a ceiling and reverses past it (§5.1, §6.4).
- **C3 — The subtraction.** Symbolic structure, in gating and in supervision, consistently costs accuracy; the cost vanishes at an exact floor (§5.2–5.3).
- **C4 — The interference floor and its exchange rate.** Trained-arm error decomposes into prior error plus a regime-mixing-dependent head noise; five interventions and a decode rule lie on one ROC curve, which we quote (§6).
- **C5 — Methodology as a working instrument.** Preregistered criteria, single-source numbers bound by guard tests, and a measured noise floor caught three of our own wrong conclusions before publication (§3.4, §9).

## 2. Related Work

**Reuse-and-correct methods.** A family of systems couples an analytic simulator with a learned corrector: alternating neural integrators for legacy simulators [ani2026], differentiable-simulator augmentation [neuralsim2021], residual physics for throwing [tossingbot2019], material-aware residual world models for deformable objects [physcore2026], and end-to-end identification of rigid-body dynamics from vision [pinwm2025]. These works answer *how to repair*. We ask the adjacent question that their evaluations presuppose: *what determines whether the repair is possible, and what the repair costs*. Our interference result is a caveat their architectures inherit — the corrector's noise on the prior's home regime — and our decode-time rule is one measurable answer.

**Structure-preserving learning.** Hamiltonian and Lagrangian networks build the integrator into the learner [hgn2019, chnn2020, se3hode2021]. The concern for long-horizon stability is the same as ours; the causal question is different. We do not ask whether a learned model can be made to respect structure, but how much of closed-loop error is explained by the prior's computation class once structure is present as a candidate explanation and rejected.

**Rollout error accumulation.** That autoregressive error compounds is a known and much-analysed property of learned dynamics [pderefiner, mbpo2019, swazinna2021]. We contribute the controlled version: with the architecture and data fixed, the compounding term is governed by the prior's fidelity, it saturates at the floor, and the training-side levers that reduce one component of it reliably inflate another (§6.4).

**Auxiliary-task interference.** Capacity loss from auxiliary losses [lyle2021], gradient-magnitude balancing across tasks [metabalance2022], and expert-utilisation pathology in mixture-of-experts [deepseekv3, auxlossfree2024] are the nearest neighbours of our interference floor. The instance here is new — a residual head that will not stay silent where an exact prior leaves it nothing to do — and we test the obvious fixes causally rather than proposing another balancer.

**Handing authority back to a deterministic floor.** Handing authority to a fallback controller is established in control: predictive safety filters verify a backup trajectory online and revert to it when a learned policy risks constraint violation [wabersich2021], and control-barrier-function filters modulate a nominal controller to keep a safe set invariant [ames2019]; abstention and conformal wrappers extend the idea under uncertainty. Our decode-time gate is that idea applied to a dynamics world model, with two additions: the regime indicator is physically interpretable (a contact indicator, not an epistemic score), and the cost of handback on in-distribution performance is measured as an explicit exchange rate rather than left implicit.

## 3. Experimental Platform

### 3.1 Two worlds

**Pendulum.** An underactuated double pendulum in the acrobot configuration: two links (L=80 px, point masses 2.0 and 1.0), gravity 900 px/s², one normalised elbow torque bounded at 28,000. Ground truth integrates the Lagrangian equations with RK4 at 0.01 s and samples at 20 Hz. The state is 25 dimensions per window step: two 11-dim object segments (position, angle as sin/cos, linear and angular velocity, constant attributes) plus a three-channel contact block. Three thousand episodes of 10 s. The prior is torque-driven and contact-free by construction, which makes this world the clean instrument: its exact tier can be checked against the engine frame by frame.

**Ball.** A planar arena simulated with a rigid-body engine: three dynamic bodies, static obstacles in eight visible slots, gravity 900 px/s², actions recorded as per-body net impulses over each 0.05 s interval, applied as constant forces at every 0.01 s engine step. Fifty thousand episodes of 200 frames. The state assembles to 116 dimensions (33 object, 80 obstacle, 3 contact). This world adds what the pendulum lacks: contact. Its free-flight segments are the only place where a contact-free prior can be exact, and its engine turned out to be instructive in its own right — the documentation describes a semi-implicit Euler step, but the data carry the signature of an explicit one (position increments of vΔt + 10aΔt² per substep, not 15aΔt²). We rebuilt the priors to match the engine as measured, and the discrepancy itself is worth a sentence: at 900 px/s², the difference between those two signatures is 0.45 px per transfer, almost three times the finest ladder gap we resolve in §4.2 (0.159 px). Documentation and code disagree at exactly the scale of interest. All quantities are in the engine's native pixel units; converting them would add a constant to every tier and cannot change a comparison, so we do not.

### 3.2 Architecture family

All arms share a GRU trunk (hidden 256 unless stated) with LayerNorm, consuming the assembled window (L=8 past frames). The residual parameterisation is fixed throughout: the head predicts a correction to the prior's output, pred = prior(sₜ, aₜ) + head(hₜ, aₜ), with the head's final layer small-initialised so that training starts at the prior. Around this trunk we register fifteen arms for the pendulum (pure learning, strong MLP, JEPA-style, gated, ablations, four analytic tiers) and the trained ladder plus two silence mechanisms for the ball. Parameter budgets are levelled to within ±10% of the base arm; the wiring of every arm is asserted from its state dictionary, not from its configuration, by guard tests [§9]. Model sizes range from 401k to 598k parameters.

### 3.3 The prior registry

Each world has a registry with four prior tiers — for the pendulum, semi-implicit Euler at 0.05 s, Euler at 0.01×5, RK4 at 0.05 s, RK4 at 0.01×5; for the ball, the legacy closed-form parabola, explicit Euler at 0.05×1, RK4 at 0.05×1, explicit Euler at 0.01×5. The registry is the single implementation: a numpy path builds training targets, a torch path serves evaluation, and a test asserts the two agree to within 10⁻⁴ and that the default tier reproduces the historical implementation bit for bit. The tier used to build the residual target during training is the same tier the model evaluates with; a mismatch there would silently invalidate every comparison, so it is guarded, not promised.

### 3.4 Protocol

Every experiment in this paper ran under a preregistration written before the first run: design, seeds, thresholds with their calibration source, stopping rules, and what counts as a crashed run. Comparisons are paired by seed; crashed runs stay in the analysis (ITT) and their rate is reported, with one preregistered exception: the gating experiments of §5 additionally report stable-run means with collapsed runs excluded, because a collapsed run's saved checkpoint is systematically worse than a completed one's, and the preregistration requires both readings to be compared and flags any disagreement. The run-to-run noise floor was measured once, by retraining one mid-tier configuration from identical settings on all three seeds and comparing to the first runs — ≤5% on one-step error, ≤9% closed-loop — and we do not claim any difference below 10%. All numbers descend from stored JSON reports through a single build script; 40 guard tests (prior-registry cross-checks, document-to-JSON consistency, checkpoint-naming and overwrite protection, wiring assertions read back from state dictionaries) make divergence between the paper's numbers and the artifacts a test failure rather than a possibility. Eleven preregistrations accompany the paper. Three of our own earlier conclusions were overturned inside this protocol, and we describe two of those overturnings in the body (§5.1, §6.4) because they are part of the result.

## 4. The Prior Ladder Sets the Floor

### 4.1 A ladder in the pendulum world

We first trained the residual architecture on the pendulum world with four priors, everything else fixed (60 epochs, three seeds). One-step error follows the prior tier monotonically: 0.969, 0.196, 0.136, 0.037 px as the integrator moves from coarse Euler to the engine's own RK4 scheme; closed-loop error at five steps follows it from 14.26 to 0.49 px 〔E0_report, P0a〕. The exact tier beats the preregistered thresholds on all four criteria. Two details deserve emphasis. The residual head's contribution to closed-loop error is not stable — across tiers it ranges from a 10% improvement over its own prior to a 50% degradation — while the prior tiers themselves differ by 32× (the trained arms, 29×). And the exact tier removes the seed variance almost entirely: closed-loop standard deviation across seeds falls from 1.3–2.4 px across the middle tiers to 0.01 px at the exact one. When the prior is right, the trained arm stops being a random variable.

### 4.2 The zero-parameter ladder crosses engines

The trained ladder conflates two things: the quality of the prior and whatever the head does with it. So we removed the head. Four zero-parameter priors were evaluated on the ball world's contact-free transfers (6,597 transfers passing a contact-flag and velocity-consistency filter), with the pendulum's three-tier analytic validation as precedent 〔S1_ball_ladder〕.

The engine check comes first and it is the reason to trust everything after it. The ball engine's exact tier — explicit Euler at 0.01 s, five substeps, constant per-step forces — reproduces the recorded transitions to 3×10⁻⁵ px one-step, and rollouts over five closed-loop steps reach 1.4×10⁻⁴ px. A zero-parameter prior is bit-exact against a learned data-generating process at deployment sampling. [Fig 1]

The ladder itself, on free-flight windows: the legacy closed-form parabola and the RK4 tier tie at 0.1591 px; the same-family coarse tier sits at 0.6364 px. Both values were predicted analytically before the run: the parabola and RK4 are the exact solution of the constant-acceleration problem and miss the engine's per-transfer position gain by 2.5aΔt² along the gravity axis (0.225 px at a=900), the coarse tier by 10aΔt² (0.9 px); pooled over the six position dimensions, of which gravity moves three, the predicted pos-RMSE is these over √2 — 0.1591 and 0.6364 px — and the measurements reproduce them to the fourth decimal. Two readings matter. First, the mathematically exact solution is not the computationally matched one: the parabola solves the ODE perfectly and is still 0.159 px wrong at every step, because the engine's discrete map is not the flow. Second, matching the family without matching the step size does not help; the same-family tier is the worst of the four. On mixed windows that include contact, all four tiers compress to 0.9–1.2 px, which is the contact mismatch asserting itself — a second, additive error source that no integrator choice addresses.

### 4.3 The aliasing law, across seeds

The identification experiments (§6 uses their architecture) put a number on what §4.2 implies. A Nelder–Mead identifier, given the ball engine's data with gravity perturbed by +25% and asked to fit the prior's parameters under a multi-step rollout objective, returns ĝ = 706 — the same value, to the unit, on three independently generated datasets 〔S2_multiseed_verdict〕. Not 900, not 1125: the identifier converges to the effective parameter of the prior's computation class, and that parameter is a deterministic function of the mismatch, not of the data sample. Refine the integrator and the estimate walks to 957–961 — most of the way home from 706, though still 15% below the generating value. Lengthen the identification horizon and it walks monotonically in every seed. The practical form of this law is a specification: an identification or filtering pipeline that does not model the prior's computation class is fitting the mismatch, and it will do so reproducibly.

### 4.4 What the floor buys

With the exact prior in place, the residual head has nothing measurable to learn in the pendulum world: the trained arm reaches 0.037 px against its prior's 0.038, and the closed-loop values are 0.49 against 0.49 〔E0_report〕. A 401k-parameter learned model ends where a zero-parameter one begins. We treat this as the clean statement of C1: the floor is real, it is set by computation rather than physics, and in a contact-free world it is the whole story. The rest of the paper is about what happens when it is not.

## 5. Structure and Recipe Cannot Substitute

### 5.1 The recipe multiplies within the floor

The pendulum arms in §4.1 were all trained single-step. If closed-loop failure is a training problem, a rollout loss should fix it. The preregistered answer took four arms across three recipe cells — single-step, closed-loop loss at the original learning rate, and closed-loop loss with the rate halved — and the gains arrange themselves exactly along the distance to the floor 〔P0b{A,B,C}〕. The collapsing arm (pure GRU, closed-loop 53.89 px) improves 41× under the rollout loss. The coarse-prior arm improves 2.9×. The arm that was already stable improves by 0%. The exact-prior arm was not run in the third cell, by preregistration, because there was nothing for the loss to act on. The best non-exact arm in any cell, 1.31 px, remains 2.7× above the exact arm's 0.49.

The cell design earned its keep by accident. The original two-cell plan (with the learning rate halved alongside the rollout loss) would have credited the loss for a degradation that belonged to the learning rate: single-step error rose in the halved-rate cell for every arm. The third cell separated the two, and the corrected reading — the rollout loss does not damage one-step accuracy at all — is one of the three self-corrections this protocol produced.

### 5.2 Symbolic structure subtracts

The gated arm — 64 phase-gated experts modulating the residual head through FiLM — has now been tested across four experiment generations: the unified main table, a ten-seed gating ablation, a capacity-decoupled variant, and a paired meta-analysis over seven gating variants. The gating term is a cost in all of them: +0.161 px paired, z = 7.14, direction consistent in 7/7 variants — variants that share seeds and architecture, so we read the count as one replicated finding rather than seven independent ones — with an expert-occupancy audit showing 89% of windows routed to three of eight phase classes and two phase classes never occupied 〔E1a_stageB, E0_report〕. A free (unstructured) gate costs the same, which rules out mis-chosen semantics as the culprit.

The 2×2 in Table [t-p0c] separates the other channel, supervision, from capacity. Holding the architecture fixed and switching only loss weights, the label-supervised variants (C10, C11) are worse than the unsupervised ones (C00, C01) by 0.269 px paired, 6/6 seeds, p = 0.011; the self-supervised prototype family contributes nothing measurable (+0.022 px). The supervision check confirms the mechanism was live — the gated arm's indicator accuracy is 0.968 against 0.474 for the unsupervised cells — so the cost is paid by a head that demonstrably learned what we asked it to learn. The reading we stand behind: phase labels are a description the state head does not need, and asking the trunk to predict them taxes the prediction we actually want.

### 5.3 The harm is absorbed by the floor

One more experiment closes the 2×2's logic. Run the exact-prior arm with symbolic structure attached and the harm disappears: at the exact tier, the gated variant lands within 0.0001 px of the ungated one (paired ratio 0.0005), while the gate's occupancy pathology is unchanged — the same 6 of 64 experts alive, the same dead classes 〔S0b_compare〕. The structure still wastes capacity; the waste no longer costs anything, because the residual head has nothing to be wrong about. This is why the subtraction result and the floor result are one result: the harm of structure is a tax on the residual signal, and the residual signal is bounded by the floor.

## 6. The Trained Arm: Interference and the Price of Silence

### 6.1 The interference floor

The ball world's trained ladder should, if §4 holds for trained arms, order itself like the analytic ladder. It does not 〔S1b_report〕. Four trained arms — same architecture, same data, priors differing by the analytic ladder of §4.2 — land at 0.86, 0.87, 0.99, 0.86 px on free-flight windows, unordered and indistinguishable, when their priors sit at 0.16, 0.64, 0.16 and 0.00003. The trained arm of the exact prior is roughly 3×10⁴ times worse than its own prior on the windows where that prior is perfect.

The probe in Table [t-probe] locates the error. On free-flight windows the residual target for the exact-prior arm is 0.0000 (normalized RMS, four decimals); the head outputs 0.0197. The coarse-prior arm's target on the same windows is 0.0036 — the analytic 0.159 px, as §4.2 predicts — and its head outputs 0.0203. The head emits the same noise regardless of what it is asked, and that noise is 5.6× the largest free-flight target present. We call the quantity the *interference floor*: a noise term in the trained arm's error, set not by the prior but by the training mixture. In the pendulum world the mixture is all free-flight, the head's target is zero everywhere, and the head learns silence — which is why §4.1's ladder is visible there. In the ball world, contact windows carry the loss, and silence is not learned. By *silence* we mean the head's quiescence on the regime where the prior is already exact — output near zero where the target is near zero — and the rest of this section is an attempt to purchase it.

### 6.2 Reweighting buys one-step silence; an end-to-end gate starves

The first intervention reweights the state loss on contact-flag-free windows (both the last input frame and the target frame contact-free) by a fixed ×10, chosen before the run. It works on the term it acts on: free-flight one-step error falls from 0.856 to 0.461 px, the head's free-flight output RMS halves (0.0186 → 0.0095), and — the part we did not expect — mixed error does not degrade (0.570 → 0.547, a 4% shift inside the 10% claim floor), so the contact regime paid nothing 〔S1c_report〕. Closed-loop free-flight error falls from 2.042 to 1.406 but misses its preregistered threshold (1.02). The second intervention adds a dedicated pathway: a per-sample gate between head and prior, trained end-to-end. It fails for a measurable reason. The regime mask is live on about 4.5% of training windows (the stricter velocity-consistent subset that the evaluation scores is 0.4%); the gradient that would teach the gate to close is outnumbered twenty to one, and the gate stays at its initialisation. Silence is purchasable with loss mass; a pathway alone is not.

### 6.3 A supervised gate learns the regime but not the silence

If starvation is the problem, supervise the gate directly: the regime label exists in the data as the contact flag, so we trained the gate with a binary cross-entropy against it, one preregistered arm 〔S1d_report〕. The gate learned the regime almost perfectly — 99.34% of windows, 95.5% of free-flight windows closed. The silence did not follow: free-flight one-step error landed at 0.545 px, closed-loop at 1.596, both worse than plain reweighting. The probe explains it. The gate is a soft attenuator — its mean on free-flight windows is 0.13 to 0.29, never zero — and the state loss constrains only the product of gate and residual. The head scaled its output up by 1.6–1.9× to compensate, and the product, which is all that training sees, moved less than reweighting had moved it. Gate and head co-adapt. Two interventions, two lessons, one conclusion forming: the loss geometry, not the architecture, is the causal variable.

### 6.4 Loss mass has a dose–response ceiling, and it reverses

The third preregistration mapped the dose. Three arms held the exact prior and varied which loss term carried the extra mass: closed-loop loss ×10 (one-step untouched by design), both terms ×10, and single-step ×100. The two-component structure of the error shows up cleanly. Closed-loop mass attacks exactly the compounding term — closed-loop falls 24.9% while the one-step free-flight error moves 1.8% (0.856 → 0.872, inside the noise floor) and mixed one-step rises 0.570 → 0.602, inside its preregistered 0.628 px bound. Both terms together give the best closed-loop of the six arms (1.329 px) at a small one-step cost against its ×10 single-step reference (0.613 against 0.461 px). And the single-step arm delivers the best free-flight one-step error of the study, 0.306 px, while its closed-loop error *reverses* to 2.727 px — 34% worse than the unmodified baseline and 94% worse than the ×10 arm it was derived from. The per-step profile is the mechanism: its error grows 2.6–3.9× per feedback step and then 4.5–5.1× on the next, in every seed, accelerating with depth, where no other arm's second-step growth exceeds 2.6×. Given enough single-step mass, the optimizer produced a head that is accurate once and unstable under its own feedback. We had the dose–response curve backwards in our preregistered expectation, and the reversal, not the improvement, is the finding: the two error components do not trade against each other monotonically, because the head is shared and the loss geometry overfits.

### 6.5 Decode-time hard gating redeems the closed loop, at a quoted rate

The four training-side interventions share a failure mode: they act through the shared head, so whatever they buy on free-flight windows is paid for on contact windows, and the exchange is not under control. The fifth intervention steps out of training entirely. At decode time, per rollout step, if the maximum contact-indicator probability over objects exceeds a threshold τ, the head is used; below it, the head is skipped and the analytic prior acts alone. The threshold is selected on the first half of the validation windows, under a rule that bounds false silencing at 2%, and all verdicts are computed on the second half 〔S1f_report〕. One preregistered detail deviates in the shipped artifacts: the τ grid was registered at 0.50–0.99 in steps of 0.01 and shipped at 0.50–0.95 in steps of 0.05.

The result closes the arc. Free-flight closed-loop error under the rule is 0.0001 px, in every seed, for the trained arms and for the unmodified baseline alike — parity with the analytic prior, from a trained model, with no retraining. Two qualifications keep the claim honest. The scored free-flight windows are few: the second validation half contains 15, 4 and 11 free-flight closed-loop windows across the three seeds — some thirty in all — so the claim is that the rule silenced every window it scored, and the heavier evidence sits in the threshold scan that selects τ (22–26 free-flight windows per seed per threshold, and some 72,000 free-flight window-steps). And the rule does not redeem every arm: the dose-reversed ×100 arm's indicator inherits its degraded head, 4–22% of its free-flight windows stay open at τ=0.9, and the few open windows carry RMS 0.23–1.55 px — silence presupposes a head whose errors are confined to the contact regime, and that arm no longer meets the premise. The indicator needs no training because it was always trained: the contact head's free-flight output probability is 0.040 on average against 0.973 on contact windows, and the τ rule merely reads it out. The cost is quoted with the same precision. The 2% of contact windows falsely silenced lose the head's correction, and contact-regime closed-loop error rises by a factor of 1.12–1.21 across arms, missing the ×1.10 tolerance we preregistered. Training the indicator for a better curve (two arms, preregistered) moved the protection rate by 0.8 percentage points — inside the noise floor — and left the curve where it was.

### 6.6 One curve, one exchange rate

Put the five interventions and the decode rule on one plot [Fig 4] and they do not scatter: they lie on a trade-off between free-flight silence and contact damage. Reweighting and the dose curve move along it from inside training; the decode rule reaches its far end from outside, by construction, and is the only point that achieves analytic parity at all. The exchange rate is the summary, and we give it as a measurement rather than an adjective: along the decode-rule scan, the false-silence percentage points paid per decade of free-flight closed-loop error purchased. On the baseline arm, moving τ from 0.5 to the silence floor buys 3.6–4.0 decades (0.40–0.81 px down to 10⁻⁴ px) for 0.6–1.2 percentage points of false silence (per seed: 1.23, 0.71, 0.55 — the floor arrives early where the indicator separates cleanly); the preregistered operating point — the largest τ whose false-silence rate stays at or below 2% — sits at 1.9–2.0% with the floor reached. From the unmasked baseline the whole purchase is 4.3 decades (2.04 → 10⁻⁴ px). Whether a better regime indicator could shift the curve itself remains open — our one attempt moved it by less than the noise floor — but a practitioner choosing a deployment point no longer needs to guess the price.

## 7. Discussion

**A specification for hybrid deployment.** The practitioner's form of C1 is a checklist item: when a world model operates next to a simulator, the simulator's computation class — integrator, step size, action sampling — must match the data-generating process at the scale being evaluated, or the floor the model inherits is the mismatch, not the physics. Our aliasing result sharpens this from advice to measurement: a fitted parameter that lands reproducibly off the true value (ĝ = 706 against 900, identical across datasets) is not an estimation failure but a property of the class, and refining the class moves it in the direction the law predicts. A reader may still object that, in simulation, matching the generating integrator is tautological. The matching is easy to state and hard to know: the engine is a known discrete map only to someone who reads its code, and ours self-describes as the wrong integrator (§3.1) — nothing in the pipeline but the data reveals that. With real sensor data there is no code to read at all, and the identification law of §4.3 is the honest analogue: fit the class, not the flow.

**Interference is not specific to this architecture.** We found the floor with a GRU residual head and mapped it with five interventions, but the mechanism — a shared head trained on a regime-imbalanced mixture will not be silent on the minority regime — is a statement about loss geometry, and the balancing literature has met its cousins in other clothes [lyle2021, metabalance2022]. The measured exchange rate is the part we believe generalises first: any proposal to make a residual head regime-quiet should be asked what it pays on the majority regime, in the currency of the metric that matters. We have staked the question, not the number: every measurement here comes from one architecture family in two planar worlds, and the exchange rate is what the next architecture should be asked to reproduce, not evidence that it will.

**What remains of the symbolic programme.** Phase labels failed as gating signal, failed as supervision, and their occupancy pathology is structural. What survives is the audit use: the indicator that the decode rule reads is a trained phase head, and the occupancy table diagnosed the gating pathology before any accuracy number did. Cultural priors, in our experience, earn their place as instruments, not components.

**Discipline as an error-correction mechanism.** Three conclusions in this paper's history died inside the protocol: a gating advantage that was two negative arms compared to each other, a loss-quality claim that a third cell attributed to the learning rate, and a dose–response expectation that the reversal disproved. Each died cheaply, before publication, because the criteria were written first and the numbers had one source. We consider the protocol the second result of the paper.

## 8. Limitations

*Scale and domain.* Two simulated worlds — a one-degree-of-freedom pendulum and a planar ball arena — with models between 401k and 598k parameters. Every claim is scoped to these. The interference mechanism and the aliasing law are statements about loss geometry and computation classes that we expect to travel, but nothing here tests them at scale, in three dimensions, or on hardware.

*Sample size.* Trained comparisons use three to six seeds, paired. The guard is direction consistency and preregistered absolute thresholds, not significance claims on small differences; the measured noise floor (5–9%) forbids those anyway.

*The silence line ends at a trade-off.* We did not achieve full closed-loop silence with training alone, and the one indicator-training attempt moved the ROC by less than the noise floor. The decode rule is a deployment answer with a quoted cost, not a training answer.

*Mechanism status.* The interference floor is measured, not derived; we have no theory that predicts its magnitude from the regime ratio. The subtraction result's cause (why labels tax the trunk) is unproven; we separated it from capacity and measured its size.

*Protocol scope.* Validation splits vary per seed (paired analysis only), windows-per-epoch was capped at 150k for cost, and the closed-loop evaluation window is five steps (pendulum) and three steps (ball) — short by design, since the pendulum's chaos saturates point predictions beyond it. The run-to-run noise floor is estimated from a single retrained configuration (§3.4); floors may differ across arms, and the free-flight windows that the decode rule scores are scarce enough that its headline number rests on some thirty windows (§6.5).

## 9. Reproducibility Statement

The repository ships the two world generators with their validation suites, the full training and evaluation pipeline, eleven preregistration documents, 40 guard tests (prior-registry cross-implementation checks, document-to-JSON number consistency, checkpoint naming and overwrite protection, architecture wiring assertions), and a manifest of model checkpoints with SHA-256 hashes. Every number in this paper is produced by a named script from a stored JSON in `analysis_output/`; the appendix table lists the command for each. All trained arms run on a single 8 GB consumer GPU; the full experimental matrix is roughly forty GPU-hours. Preregistrations were committed before the first run of each experiment, and the two exceptions to strict pairing (per-seed validation splits, fixed identification windows) are declared in the preregistrations themselves. One preregistered detail deviates in the shipped artifacts: the S1f threshold grid was registered at 0.50–0.99 in steps of 0.01 and shipped at 0.50–0.95 in steps of 0.05.
